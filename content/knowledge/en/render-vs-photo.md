"Why not just photograph it?" is a fair question. Sometimes a photo shoot really is simpler. Here's how to tell where 3D pays off and where it doesn't.

## What wins when

::kb-figure{art="render-or-photo" alt="Six situations and what wins in each: no product yet — 3D; many colors and configurations — 3D; a series in one style — 3D; show what is inside — 3D; people and a live scene — photo; one shot, sample at hand — photo."}
A filled tag means 3D, an outlined one means photo.
::

**3D wins:**

- **No product yet** — only drawings or [CAD](/knowledge/glossary#cad). The render is ready before the first batch.
- **Many colors, fabrics, configurations.** One model; the variant is switched with the material.
- **A series.** The same light, background and angles for every item, and a new item lines up without a reshoot.
- **The inside on show.** Exploded views and cutaways a camera can't capture — more in [Exploded view](/knowledge/exploded-view).
- **Large or awkward equipment** — a machine, a boiler, a production line that won't fit in a studio.

**Photos are better:**

- **People, emotion, a live setting** — a lifestyle shoot with models.
- **One or two shots**, the sample at hand, and no variants coming.
- **"Living" materials** — food, cosmetics on skin: a photo is usually more honest and simpler.

One doesn't rule out the other: the catalog is often rendered, and lifestyle is shot on camera.

## One color or six

::kb-figure{art="photo-vs-render-variants" alt="Two rows. Photo: six samples in different colors, each with its own camera. 3D: one model with six color variants coming out of it."}
For photos, every color is a separate sample and a separate shoot. In 3D it's one model.
::

A photo shoot needs a separate sample and a separate shoot for every color. In 3D it's one model: the color is changed with the material, and all variants come out with the same light and angle.

## How the amount of work grows

::kb-figure{art="effort-curve" alt="A chart with no numbers: the horizontal axis is the number of variants and angles, the vertical axis is the amount of work. The photo line rises steadily; the 3D line starts higher, with the model, and then rises gently. The lines cross."}
No numbers on purpose: where the lines cross depends on the product.
::

The main work in 3D is the model itself. After that, each new angle, color or configuration is a relatively small revision. With photos, each new shot is almost as much work as the first. Where the lines cross depends on the product: the more variants and the longer the catalog lives, the more likely 3D comes out ahead.

## Can buyers tell a render from a photo?

With well-tuned materials and lighting, a catalog render on a neutral background looks like a studio photo. What matters more is that the render matches the product the buyer receives. How to get there is covered in [How to specify color and material](/knowledge/color-and-materials).

## What it looks like in practice

- [Kiparis](/projects/kiparis): more than 100 furniture models, some in 6–8 upholstery variants. A new item joins the catalog without a reshoot.
- [KERMI](/projects/klivet): 15 radiator models in [RAL](/knowledge/glossary#ral) colors and in white — more than fifty catalog renders.
- [Nitrogen](/projects/nitrogen): large installations that won't fit in a photo studio — renders on a transparent background for the catalog and the website.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing one render with one photo: 3D pays off on variants and series.
- Expecting a live scene with people from a catalog render.
- Ordering a render without color samples and getting a different shade.

## Checklist

1. Does the product physically exist?
2. How many colors and configurations — now and later?
3. Is this a series that will keep growing?
4. Do you need to show how it's built?
5. Do you need people and a live setting — that's a job for a photographer.
